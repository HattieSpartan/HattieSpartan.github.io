A spam filter built from first principles

Naive Bayes written by hand, tested on unseen messages, and checked against scikit-learn, which exposed a bias I then diagnosed.

01 · ProblemUnderstand the model, not just call it

Spam filtering is a classic classification problem, and a library can solve it in one line. I wanted to understand exactly how the decision gets made, so I wrote a Naive Bayes classifier by hand: word counts, prior probabilities and smoothing, with no library doing the maths.

The dataset: 5,572 SMS messages labelled spam or legitimate (“ham”), about 13% spam.

02 · ApproachBuild it, then test it honestly

  • Cleaning: lowercase, remove punctuation, numbers and common stop words.
  • Honest split: 20% of messages held out before any word was counted, stratified so both sets keep the same spam share.
  • The classifier: for each message, combine the prior probability of spam with how likely each word is under spam versus ham.
  • Two practical fixes: Laplace smoothing, so an unseen word doesn’t force a probability of zero, and adding log-probabilities instead of multiplying tiny numbers, which would otherwise round down to zero.
  • A benchmark: scikit-learn’s MultinomialNB, trained on the same data, as a correctness check.

03 · AnalysisResults on 1,115 unseen messages

Metric (test set)Hand-builtscikit-learn
Accuracy96.8%97.9%
Spam precision84.2%–
Spam recall93.3%–

The model caught 139 of 149 spam messages. But it also flagged 26 legitimate messages as spam, and for a spam filter that is the costly mistake: a real message hidden where the user won’t look.

The two models agreed on 97.9% of messages, yet scikit-learn was about a point more accurate. So I went looking for the difference.

04 · Result & insightThe benchmark exposed a bias in my model

The gap came from words that never appeared in training. scikit-learn ignores them. My model still applied a small smoothed probability to each one, and because spam messages contain far fewer words overall, that probability was larger on the spam side. Every unfamiliar word nudged a message towards spam, which is exactly why most of my errors were false positives.

Why this is the part I’m proudest of

The 96.8% looked fine on its own. Comparing against a trusted reference is what revealed the flaw. The fix (skip words outside the training vocabulary) is one line.

05 · Why it mattersWhere this thinking applies

  • Not all errors cost the same. A filter that hides real customer messages does more damage than one that lets some spam through; precision on the spam class deserves as much attention as accuracy.
  • Explainability: Naive Bayes can say exactly which words pushed a message over the line, which matters when a decision has to be justified.
  • Validate against a reference. Any in-house model or report can be checked this way before people rely on it.
