A Comparative Evaluation of Classical Actuarial and Statistical Learning Methods for Claims Reserving in Kenyan Short-Tail General Insurance

In progress · concept paper submitted September 2026 · expected completion 2027

The problem

When an insurer writes a policy, some of the claims it will pay have already happened but haven’t been reported or fully settled yet. The insurer has to set money aside for them, and that claims reserve is usually one of the largest numbers on its balance sheet. Set it too low and the insurer looks healthier than it is; set it too high and capital sits idle.

Actuaries have long estimated reserves with classical methods such as the chain ladder, which projects how claims develop over time from past patterns. These methods are transparent and well understood, but they rest on assumptions that don’t always hold. Statistical learning methods can pick up more complex patterns, but they are harder to explain, and in reserving an explanation matters as much as a number.

The question

For short-tail general insurance lines in Kenya, do statistical learning methods estimate outstanding claims more accurately than classical actuarial methods, and at what cost to transparency?

Planned approach

This is the direction set out in my concept paper. Details will change as the research develops.

  • Classical baselines: chain ladder, with Mack’s method to estimate uncertainty around the reserve.
  • Statistical learning: machine-learning models such as gradient-boosted trees, with explainability tools (e.g. SHAP) so the drivers of each estimate can be inspected.
  • Fair comparison: out-of-time validation. Hold back the most recent diagonal of the claims triangle, fit each method on the rest, and compare how well each one predicts what actually happened.
  • Data: Kenyan short-tail claims data, if access can be secured, for local relevance.

Why I chose it

It sits exactly where my two backgrounds meet. My actuarial degree taught me why reserving matters and how the classical methods work; my data science training gives me the tools to test whether newer methods genuinely do better, rather than assuming they do. Kenyan insurers also report under IFRS 17, which puts more weight on well-supported estimates of what is owed on past claims.
